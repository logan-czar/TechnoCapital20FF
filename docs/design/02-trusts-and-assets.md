# 2. Trusts, accounts and assets ("Wall Street Raiders Lite")

## 2.1 Characters are landless; assets are titles

Nobody holds a county. Every character is landless. What they hold instead is **assets**: stakes in companies, real estate, bonds, accounts. Assets play the role of titles:

- **Standing**: a character's tier comes from the total value they control: *Investor*, *Magnate*, *Mogul*, *Tycoon*, *Oligarch*.
- **Authority**: controlling a company makes you its head under its charter.
- **Influence**: assets concentrated in a place give influence there (see [05-map-and-influence.md](05-map-and-influence.md)).

## 2.2 The family trust (replaces gold)

The **family trust** is the dynasty's core wallet and portfolio, and the thread that carries the dynasty through a whole playthrough.

- It is governed by **set rules** that persist across the playthrough: the succession law, eligibility, distribution policy, and any restrictions (for example "never sell the flagship"). Changing them needs a family vote and costs opinion.
- The dynasty head is its **trustee** and directs it. Family members are **beneficiaries**.
- When the head dies, trusteeship passes by the trust's succession law (see [04-dynasty-and-succession.md](04-dynasty-and-succession.md)). Assets stay in the trust.
- The player loses when no dynasty member is trustee.

## 2.3 Personal trusts and accounts

Characters can also hold assets **outside** the family trust, in **personal accounts** and **personal trusts**. These are free-form: no family rules apply.

- A character can buy, sell, borrow, gamble and hide money however they like.
- Personal assets pass by **will** on death, not by the trust's succession law. Wills can be contested, forged (scheme) or changed under pressure (hooks).
- Personal holdings let heirs, spouses and rivals within the family build their own power base: an heir with a large personal stake in the flagship can form a bloc against the trustee.
- The player directly manages the dynasty head's personal accounts and the family trust. Other family members manage their own, driven by AI and traits.

## 2.4 Cash flow

Each month, every trust and account:

- **Earns**: dividends, rent, interest, coupons, salaries of members who work as executives.
- **Spends**: lifestyle costs, distributions to beneficiaries, scheme costs, loan interest, staff, taxes (depending on jurisdiction and shells).

Underpaid beneficiaries lose opinion of the trustee and become candidates for rival blocs or a contested succession.

## 2.5 Asset classes

| Asset | How it works | Yield and risk | Influence |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Company stakes** | Shares in public or private companies | Dividends plus price moves; voting power | Through the company's facilities and jobs in an area |
| **Real estate** | Residential, commercial, industrial, land; located in a hub | Rent plus appreciation; illiquid (sells over weeks) | Strong local influence |
| **Cash account** | Instant access | None | None |
| **Savings / money market** | Low risk | Low yield, tied to the central bank rate | None |
| **Government bonds** | Lend to a country, state or city | Medium yield; default risk | Political influence with the issuer |
| **Corporate bonds** | Lend to a company | Higher yield; creditor claim on bankruptcy | Leverage over the company |
| **High-yield / private credit** | Risky lending | High yield, real chance of loss | Leverage over borrowers |
| **Commodities** | Positions in goods (see [07-markets.md](07-markets.md)) | Price moves only; storage cost for physical | None |
| **Foreign currency** | Holdings in other currencies | FX gains and losses | None |
| **Speculative** | Crypto, data futures, black-market tokens | Volatile; may require a shell | None |
| **Collectibles and luxury** | Art, yachts, private islands | Prestige and opinion boosts; illiquid | Small |

Accounts and assets are **denominated in a currency**. Holding assets abroad exposes the trust to FX moves and capital controls.

## 2.6 Trading (lite)

- One **portfolio screen** handles buying and selling across asset classes.
- Large orders move prices (slippage), so building a big stake gets more expensive.
- **Disclosure thresholds**: crossing 10%, 25% and 50% of a company is announced everywhere and holders react. Each country's securities law adds an earlier first filing line (2% to 10%, usually 5%). Buying through a shell hides it until investigated.
- Real estate and private stakes sell through a short negotiation event rather than instantly.

## 2.7 Leverage

- **Margin loans** against a portfolio; a falling portfolio triggers a **margin call** that forces sales and can break control of a key company.
- **Mortgages** against real estate.
- **Personal guarantees** on company debt: if the company defaults, the guarantor pays.
- Companies can run **leveraged buyouts**, loading debt onto the target.

## 2.8 Taxes and shells

Each jurisdiction taxes income, capital gains and inheritance at its own rates (see [08-politics.md](08-politics.md)). Routing assets through shell companies in low-tax jurisdictions cuts taxes but builds **exposure**; high exposure plus an investigation leads to fines, frozen assets and scandal.
